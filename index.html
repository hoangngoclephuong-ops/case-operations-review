Section 1

Executive summary

Five findings from 599 customer cases. Each is cross-referenced to the section containing the supporting analysis.

  1. Completion time is now measurable end to end. Measured from assignment to the visa application reaching Home Affairs it covers 93% of cases, has a median of 36 days, and orders priority exactly as it should: urgent 9 days, high 26, medium 47, low 73. That is a clean monotonic gradient across four bands — the signature of a measure tracking real work. This is the number to manage the business to; first response (section 7) measures the visa team's own turnaround inside it. Section 6
  2. The slowest stage is the one the team controls most. Splitting those 36 days into its parts: assignment to offer letter 6 days, offer letter to CoE 7 days, then CoE to lodgement 17 days — nearly half the elapsed time sits after the provider has done its part and before the visa application goes to Home Affairs. Provider turnaround is not the constraint. Section 6
  3. First response is fast, and the values are missing rather than wrong. Once a case manager hands a case over, the visa team lodges the school application the same day in 55% of cases, within one day in 73% and within a week in 90%, with a genuine tail behind it. The problem with this field is coverage, not accuracy: it is blank on roughly half of completed cases, across the whole team rather than two individuals, which halves the sample behind the measure. Section 7
  4. Case management is the lopsided layer; execution is not. Staff B owns 35% of the cases that have a case manager recorded and the top three own 77% between them (Gini 0.57), while the visa team's eight substantive members range only 69–82 cases — a spread of 1.2× (Gini 0.04). Sales is concentrated too — Staff A originates 66% — but a consultant sells and hands over, so that is revenue concentration rather than workload. Section 3
  5. Demand swings by nearly two to one across the year. Over the twelve months to March 2026 — the only stretch with complete coverage — volume runs from 28 cases in April to 53 in January, averaging 41 a month. January, October and March are the peaks; April, December and July the troughs. On fixed headcount that is the difference between comfortable and stretched, and it is predictable enough to roster against. Section 5

Two conclusions this data does not support

The first-response measure should not be read as a ranking. It is recorded on only about half of cases, and the differences between individuals (61% to 90% within one day) sit inside that coverage gap. Fix the coverage first, then compare.

Consultant caseload should not be read as workload. The consultant sells and hands over to the monitor team. The two consultants also lodge at almost identical speed — 36 and 38 days — which is what you would expect if they are not the ones doing the work.

Operating model — the three layers

A case passes through three hands. Each role column in the source file therefore means something different, and reading them alike is the single easiest way to draw a wrong conclusion from this data.

How a case moves

Each column in the source file belongs to a different stage, and they must not be read the same way

1ConsultantSells the service and originates the case. Does not do the casework."Caseload" here = sales volume
→
2Monitor teamOwns the customer relationship, communicates, and assigns the work onward.This is the case-management load
→
3Visa teamExecutes the lodgement work.This is the execution load

The handoff is a pool, not fixed pairing — every monitor works with every visa-team member, so visa allocation is decided centrally rather than inherited from whoever sold the case. That matters for the next three cards: the same "number of cases" means three different things depending on which column it comes from.

Layer 1 · Sales — where the business comes from

Cases originated per consultant

This is not a workload chart. A consultant who originates a case has sold it once; the casework then sits with the case-management and visa teams. The concentration here is a commercial fact — key-person risk in the revenue line, and a succession question if that person is ever unavailable — not a sign that anyone is buried in files.

Layer 2 · Case management

Cases owned per monitor-team member — the real delivery load

This is the layer that is genuinely lopsided. One person owns roughly a third of every live customer relationship, while the bottom of the list holds single figures. Unlike the sales column, this is real day-to-day work — calls, chasing documents, coordinating the visa team.

Layer 3 · Execution

Cases per visa-team member

This layer is allocated well and is worth saying out loud: across the eight substantive members the busiest and quietest differ by a factor of just 1.2, and the top three hold 40% of the work against 33% under a perfectly even split (Gini 0.04). On the same measure the case-management team’s top three hold 77% against an even 27% (Gini 0.57). Whatever process distributes visa work is doing its job.

Does carrying more cases make anyone slower?

Case-management load against time from assignment to lodgement

Cases owned Median days to lodgement

The two bars are on different scales and are meant to be compared by shape, not length — if load drove delay, the orange bars would track the blue ones. They do not. The heaviest-loaded monitor sits exactly on the overall median, while lighter-loaded colleagues sit both well above and well below it. So the concentration in layer 2 is a continuity and key-person risk rather than a throughput problem — which changes what you would do about it.

The exception is January to March, and because the median case runs 36 days the January intake is still being worked through February — which is itself an above-average month, and then March again. Section 7

Speed of service

Two things are being measured here, and they answer different questions. The first is how quickly the visa team acts once a case is handed to them. The second is how long the whole case takes to complete.

Measure 1 — first response: assign → school application

When a case manager hands a case to the visa team, the school application is to go in as soon as possible. This measures whether it does.

This is a performance measure, and the team performs well on it. The concentration at zero days is the target being met, not a defect — when the documents are ready, the application goes in the same day. A real tail exists behind it (the slowest 10% take more than a week, the longest 173 days), which is what separates genuine fast turnaround from a copied date. Read the median of 0 as "the typical case goes in immediately", and manage the tail rather than the median.

The one real data gap: the submission date is often not recorded

Share of each visa-team member’s completed cases with no submission date entered

The values in that field are trustworthy; the problem is that it is blank on roughly half of completed cases, which halves the sample behind measure 1. The gap runs across the whole team rather than sitting with two people, so it is a process fix — make the field required at the point the school application is lodged — and not a conversation with individuals.

Section 8

Incomplete records

Every case in this file is finished work. Anything without a lodgement date is therefore a recording gap, not a case still in progress.

Records with no lodgement date, by case manager

Completeness by field (% of 599 cases populated)

Because the file contains only completed work, these are records to repair rather than customers to chase. The one case recording a completion time of over a year has been excluded from every duration figure on the same basis — a finished case cannot plausibly have taken that long, so the dates are wrong rather than the case being slow.

Method and tools

How this analysis was produced, and by what

Data preparation in Power Query (type handling, blank-row removal, derived stage durations, route classification); measures in DAX; the same analysis is published as an interactive Power BI report with slicers, cross-filtering and hover-level stage breakdowns. This written report was drafted with AI assistance (Claude). The domain interpretation, the analytical judgements, and the conclusions — including the corrections recorded above — are the author’s.
